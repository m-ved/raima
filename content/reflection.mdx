Going into this course, I thought I had a decent grasp of what DEI meant - diversity, inclusion, a few training modules, maybe a company poster in the break room. What I did not expect was to leave with more questions than answers, and honestly, that feels like the more honest outcome.

The biggest thing that stood out to me over these past few months is how performative DEI can be in practice. Organizations will proudly display their commitment to diversity in their mission statements and recruitment materials, yet the actual systems and decisions do not always reflect that. It made me think critically about the difference between DEI as a genuine organizational value versus DEI as a branding exercise. The question I kept coming back to was: who does this actually serve? When the answer is "the organization's image," something has gone wrong.

This tension between intention and reality also got me thinking about DEI beyond the workplace, specifically at the level of governance and national identity. I came across a video where Singapore's Prime Minister argued that India's diversity - with its many regional identities, languages, and power structures - makes unified national progress more difficult. It was a provocative claim, and I found myself both understanding the logic and disagreeing with the conclusion. Singapore operates under a very centralized, top-down model that has produced economic success, but it has also involved restrictions on political speech and civil liberties that many would find troubling. I think this raises an important DEI question: can enforced social harmony actually undermine genuine inclusion? Silencing difference is not the same as embracing it. A society that appears cohesive on the surface because dissent is discouraged is not a diverse and inclusive one - it is a controlled one. The messiness of democratic pluralism, while inefficient, may actually be closer to the spirit of DEI than a tidy but top-down alternative.

The experience that crystallized all of this for me personally happened during a job search last summer. I had made it to the final round of interviews for a Safety HR internship - multiple rounds, real time and effort invested. It was only after all of that I was told I was ineligible because I was not Hispanic. I sat with that for a while. On one hand, I understand that targeted programs exist to address historical underrepresentation of specific groups, and there is a legitimate argument for that. On the other hand, it was jarring to be screened out based on ethnicity at the final stage, especially since I am a minority myself. The question that stuck with me was not just about fairness to me; it was about process. Why was this not disclosed upfront? The lack of transparency felt like a failure of the very inclusive values the role was presumably meant to uphold.

Altogether, my engagement with DEI this semester has pushed me to think past the surface level. I came in expecting frameworks and left with a deeper skepticism - not of DEI as a value, but of how easily it can be co-opted, simplified, or selectively applied. Real inclusion requires transparency, consistency, and a willingness to sit with discomfort rather than manage appearances. That is the version of DEI I want to hold organizations, and myself, accountable to.
